# 12 · Stagnation, atoms and frontiers

Citation IDs are in `SOURCES.md`. `[NR11]`, `[FS12]`, `[NPR14]`, `[WBUR14]`, `[CWT15]`, `[NYT15]`, `[HAM16]`,
`[AMA14]` are Thiel's own words. `[WBUR14]` is a partial transcript made by a trial transcription service and
keeps spoken slips. `[CS183-nn]` are Blake Masters' class notes. **Scope:** this chapter covers his technology
argument only. His explanations of *why* stagnation happened often turn to regulation and politics; those parts
are out of scope and are only named, not argued.

## The thesis: bits, not atoms

> "There's the question of stagnation, which I think has been a story of stagnation in the world of atoms, not
> bits." [CWT15]

The 2014 radio version: "there has been a lot of progress in the world of bits and not so much in the world of
atoms." [NPR14] He lists what slowed: energy, transportation, space travel and the other technologies people
imagined in the 1960s [WBUR14]. The word technology itself, he says, now "basically just means computers"
[WBUR14].

His 2011 essay gives the shape: "The speedup in information technology contrasts dramatically with the slowdown
everywhere else." [NR11] Its example of reversal is travel speed: faster ships, trains, cars and planes for
centuries, then a reversal when the Concorde was retired in 2003 [NR11]. The 2016 version, in one line: "Computers
are getting faster and smartphones are somewhat new." [HAM16] But jets are slower and incomes are flat, he says,
so the truth is "closer to stagnation." [HAM16]

## The data point he uses (2012)

In the 2012 Fortune debate, his main data point is US median wages, which he describes as stagnant since 1973,
against a six-fold improvement from 1932 to 1972 that came with rapid technological progress (our paraphrase
[FS12]). His simplified account: "incremental but relentless progress on the computer side" and "basically no
progress on energy." [FS12]

**Our reading:** you don't have to accept the macro claim to use the business point. If he's right, the
frontiers with the fewest competitors are outside pure software.

## Big tech as a stagnation signal

The same debate is where he argues that a successful tech company can be a sign of stagnation. His claim about
Google in 2012, paraphrased: a great company with tens of billions in cash that "has no idea how to invest that
money in technology effectively." [FS12] He adds that investors in Google are betting against new search
technology, so the honest description is a search company, not a technology company [FS12]. In 2014 he made a
similar, gentler point about Apple: the iPhone 6 was an improvement but "much more of an incremental
improvement" than the first iPhone [WBUR14].

**Our reading:** treat this as a test, not a verdict on any company. A business that can't find anything new to
do with its profits has, by his logic, reached the end of its frontier. That's an opening for whoever finds the
next one.

## Cleantech: money without technology

His lesson from the 2000s cleantech boom: "while the money was there, the technology wasn't" [NYT15]. The 2012
class on cleantech reached the same verdict in more detail (chapter 11's ten-point list) [CS183-14]. In 2015 he
named the new nuclear reactor designs as the kind of hard-technology bet that had attracted "entrepreneurs and
venture capitalists like me" [NYT15]. (The rest of that op-ed is about energy policy and is not used here.)

## Where the frontiers are, in his words

- **The boundary of bits and atoms.** "I think the most natural hope is that information technology starts to
  broaden out and starts to impact this world of atoms." [CWT15] He gives self-driving cars as an example and
  says "AI feels slightly overhyped." (2015) [CWT15].
- **Biotech.** "Biotech, a lot could happen. It feels heavily regulated." [CWT15] In 2014 he named biomedicine as
  the clearest case of low expectations [WBUR14, AMA14].
- **Hard tangible companies.** Tesla and SpaceX, he says, "represent significant sort of quantum step
  improvements" over the alternatives, "And yet it's also somehow quite rare." [WBUR14]
- **Small groups, not movements.** He expects progress "in small groups in new new companies" rather than mass
  movements [WBUR14].

Why founders avoid atoms, in his words: starting companies in bits "does make sense on the level of the founders"
[AMA14]. His concern is the balance across society, not any one founder's choice [AMA14]. In 2015 he gave a cost
comparison: about $100,000 to start a software company against something like a billion dollars to get a new
drug through approval (our paraphrase [CWT15]).

## The tagline he didn't write

The line about flying cars and 140 characters is often quoted as his. In 2014 he called it a title on "our
website" [WBUR14], meaning Founders Fund. The fund's manifesto that carried the theme was bylined by Bruce Gibney
[FF11]. We don't quote the tagline as Thiel's.

## Frontiers beat disruption

The 2012 notes: "Much better than to disrupt is to find a frontier and go for it." [CS183-04, from Blake Masters'
class notes] **Our reading:** stagnation is his diagnosis for society and an opportunity map for a founder. A
field where nothing has happened for decades is, by chapter 04's logic, the place where the last great
development can still be made.

## How to apply it: a frontier scan (our reading)

The method is ours, anchored to his bits/atoms split [CWT15] and the last-development idea [CS183-04].

1. **Pick a field** you know (logistics, materials, food, energy, health).
2. **Date its last big change:** when did the core method or cost last improve by 10x?
3. **Classify it:** pure bits, pure atoms, or the boundary where software changes a physical process.
4. **List the blockers** honestly: physics, capital, regulation, customer habit. Regulation is a fact to plan
   around here, not a debate.
5. **Check the competition:** how many funded startups work on it? Fewer is better, if the need is real.
6. **Write the one-sentence frontier:** *nothing has changed in X since Y because Z; software (or a new method)
   can now do W.*

**Worked scenario (fictional, invented numbers).** A founder compares three fields. Restaurant ordering apps:
pure bits, 400 funded rivals, changed constantly. Industrial boiler maintenance: atoms, last big change in the
1990s, 6 funded startups, blocked mostly by customer habit. Concrete curing: boundary, 2 startups, blocked by
capital (test rigs at $400k). The founder picks boilers: a field that hasn't moved in 30 years, with sensors and
scheduling software now cheap enough to change the process, and a blocker that a good first market can overcome.

**Failure modes (our reading):**
- **Atoms for romance.** Hard tech with no first market and no path to revenue (the cleantech lesson [NYT15]).
- **Mistaking a slow field for a frontier.** Sometimes nothing changed because nothing needs to.
- **Blaming the blocker.** Regulation and capital are real; a plan that only works if they vanish isn't a plan.
- **Calling a software feature a frontier.** The boundary means a physical process changes.

**Limits:** his stagnation thesis is contested, and parts of his explanation are political, which this playbook
doesn't cover. The 2012 wage figures and the 2012 Google figures are dated. The scan is ours.

**Use it now:** `templates/07-frontier-scan.md`.

**Checks to run:**
1. Is your company in bits, atoms, or at the boundary? Why there?
2. When did your field last change by 10x? What would the next change be?
3. If you run a profitable business, do you know what new thing you'd build with the cash?
4. Is your blocker physics, capital, regulation or habit? Which can your first market overcome?
