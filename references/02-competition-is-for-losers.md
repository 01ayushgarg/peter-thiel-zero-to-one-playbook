# 02 · Competition is for losers

Citation IDs are in `SOURCES.md`. `[YCL5]`, `[WSJ14]`, `[NPR14]`, `[AMA14]`, `[CWT15]` are Thiel's own words.
`[CS183-nn]` are Blake Masters' class notes, not a transcript.

> "I sort of have a single idée fixe that I'm completely obsessed with on the business side which is that if
> you're starting a company, if you're the founder, entrepreneur, starting a company you always want to aim for
> monopoly and you want to always avoid competition." [YCL5]

## Create X, capture Y%

His formula for a valuable company has two parts: it creates X dollars of value for the world, and it
captures Y percent of X. The part people miss, he says, is that X and Y "are completely independent
variables, and so" X can be very big while Y is very small [YCL5].

> "Creating value isn't enough—you also need to capture some of the value you create." [WSJ14]

**Our reading:** two separate questions, never one. Is the value real and big (X)? Can you keep a share of it
(Y)? Most "great ideas" fail on Y, not on X.

## The airlines vs Google

His numbers, dated to 2012:

> "But in 2012, when the average airfare each way was $178, the airlines made only 37 cents per passenger
> trip." [WSJ14]

> "Google brought in $50 billion in 2012 (versus $160 billion for the airlines), but it kept 21% of those
> revenues as profits—more than 100 times the airline industry's profit margin that year." [WSJ14]

In the 2014 lecture he gives $195 billion of US domestic airline revenue for 2012 [YCL5], a different base from
the $160 billion in the essay. Both make the same point: "I think in the entire hundred year history of the
airline industry, the cumulative profits in the US have been approximately zero." [YCL5]

> "So you have search much smaller than air travel but much more valuable." [YCL5]

## Two kinds of business, almost nothing between

> "There is shockingly little that is in between." [YCL5]

> "Under perfect competition, in the long run no company makes an economic profit." [WSJ14]

> "Actually, capitalism and competition are opposites." [WSJ14] On radio the same week: "I think people always
> say that capitalism and competition are synonyms. But I think they're really antonyms." [NPR14]

What he means by monopoly is narrow: "the kind of company that is so good at what it does that no other firm
can offer a close substitute." [WSJ14] He excludes "illegal bullies or government
favorites" [WSJ14].

## Why monopoly matters inside the company

> "In business, money is either an important thing or it is everything." [WSJ14]

> "In perfect competition, a business is so focused on today's margins that it can't possibly plan for a
> long-term future." [WSJ14]

The restaurant owner who has to "put Grandma to work at the register and make the kids wash dishes in the
back" [WSJ14] is his picture of competition from the inside.

## The lies people tell about markets

> "So if the monopolists pretend not to have monopolies, the non-monopolies pretend to have monopolies, the
> apparent difference is very small whereas the real difference I would submit is actually quite big." [YCL5]

**Union vs intersection:**

> "So typically if you want to think of this in set theoretic terms, you could say that a monopoly tells a lie
> where you describe your business as the union of these vastly different markets and the non-monopolist
> describes it as the intersection." [YCL5]

| The lie | Who tells it | His example |
|---|---|---|
| Our market is tiny and we're alone in it (intersection) | Startups, non-monopolies | The only British restaurant in Palo Alto [WSJ14, YCL5] |
| Our market is huge and full of rivals (union) | Monopolies | Google as an ad company or a tech company [WSJ14, YCL5] |

**The intersection lie, from the inside:**

> "The fatal temptation is to describe your market extremely narrowly so that you dominate it by definition."
> [WSJ14]

> "The something of somewhere is really mostly just the nothing of nowhere, it's like the Stanford of North
> Dakota, one of a kind, but it's not Stanford." [YCL5]

The buzzword version: "you just take a whole series of the buzzwords: sharing, mobile, social apps, you
combine them and give some kind of narrative and whether or not that's a real business or not, it is
generally a bad sign." [YCL5] The Hollywood version: a college football star joins hackers to catch a shark.
The real category: "it's just another movie" [YCL5].

**The union lie, with dated numbers.** As of May 2014, Google had "about 68% of the search market" [WSJ14]
(in the lecture, "about a happy sixty-six percent" [YCL5]). Call it an ad company and it "would own just 3.4%
of the global advertising market" [WSJ14]. Call it a tech company and it "owns less than 0.24%" of consumer
tech [WSJ14]. "Framing itself as just another tech company allows Google to escape all sorts of unwanted
attention." [WSJ14]

## The Castro Street restaurants (2001)

> "In 2001, my co-workers at PayPal and I would often get lunch on Castro Street in Mountain View, Calif."
> [WSJ14]

> "In contrast to the competitive local restaurant market, PayPal was then the only email-based payments
> company in the world. We employed fewer people than the restaurants on Castro Street did, but our business
> was much more valuable than all those restaurants combined." [WSJ14]

> "Starting a new South Indian restaurant is a really hard way to make money." [WSJ14]

**The move:** describe your market the way a skeptical outsider would, then the way a monopolist would, and
see where your company actually sits.

## The one question he asks

Asked how to tell a monopoly from a non-monopoly when looking at an idea:

> "I would say the question I always focus on is what is the actual market? So not what's the narrative of the
> market, because you can always tell a fictional story about a market" [YCL5]

## Monopoly is not stasis

He separates creative monopolies from rent collectors: "Creative monopolists give customers more choices by
adding entirely new categories of abundance to the world." [WSJ14] And: "the history of progress is a history
of better monopoly businesses replacing incumbents." [WSJ14]

> "Monopoly is the condition of every successful business." [WSJ14]

> "All happy companies are different: Each one earns a monopoly by solving a unique problem. All failed
> companies are the same: They failed to escape competition." [WSJ14]

## Who captures the value: the history

X and Y being independent explains why great inventors often got nothing: "Wright brothers, you fly the
first plane, you don't make any money." [YCL5] "The railroads were incredibly valuable, they mostly just went
bankrupt because there was too much competition." [YCL5] The textile mills of 1780 to 1850 improved for decades
and "it was all competed away" [YCL5].

**Our reading:** a big X with a small Y is a good thing to do for the world and a hard business to fund. Know
which one you're building.

**Use it now:** `templates/02-market-reality-check.md`.

**Checks to run:**
1. Write your market as an intersection (the only X for Y in Z). Now drop each qualifier. Who are you
   competing with at each step?
2. What is the actual market, in dollars, that customers already spend on solving this?
3. Are you building a big X with a tiny Y? What would let you capture more?
4. If you had a monopoly, how would you describe your market to hide it? Is that description closer to the
   truth than your pitch?
