# 02 · Competition is for losers

Citation IDs are in `SOURCES.md`. `[YCL5]`, `[WSJ14]`, `[NPR14]`, `[AMA14]`, `[CWT15]`, `[FQA14]` are Thiel's
own words. `[CS183-nn]` are Blake Masters' class notes, not a transcript.

His thesis in one line: "you always want to aim for monopoly and you want to always avoid competition." [YCL5]

## Create X, capture Y%

In the 2014 lecture he splits a valuable company into two parts: it creates X dollars of value for the world,
and it captures Y percent of X. The point he stresses is that X and Y "are completely independent variables"
[YCL5], so a company can create enormous value and keep almost none of it. The essay says the same: "Creating
value isn't enough—you also need to capture some of the value you create." [WSJ14]

**Our reading:** treat them as two separate questions. Is the value real and big (X)? Can you keep a share of it
(Y)? In our experience founders test X far more carefully than Y, so Y is often where an idea is weakest. That's
our observation, not his claim.

## The airlines vs Google

His numbers, dated to 2012 [WSJ14]: the average one-way airfare was $178, yet the airlines "made only 37 cents
per passenger trip." Google brought in about $50 billion that year against $160 billion for the airlines, but kept
21% as profit, more than 100 times the airline margin (our paraphrase of the essay's figures).

In the 2014 lecture he uses $195 billion of US domestic airline revenue for 2012 [YCL5], a different base from
the essay. Both make the same point, and he adds that over the industry's history the "cumulative profits in the
US have been approximately zero." [YCL5]

## Two kinds of business, almost nothing between

Perfect competition and monopoly are the two poles, and "There is shockingly little that is in between." [YCL5]
Under perfect competition, the essay says, "in the long run no company makes an economic profit." [WSJ14] His
summary of the whole idea: "Actually, capitalism and competition are opposites." [WSJ14]

What he means by monopoly is narrow: a company "so good at what it does that no other firm can offer a close
substitute." [WSJ14] He excludes "illegal bullies or government favorites" [WSJ14]. In the 2012 Fortune debate
he made the legal line explicit: "it's quite legal to have a monopoly as long as you don't abuse it." [FS12]

**Why it matters inside the company.** The essay's picture is a competitive restaurant where the whole family
works the register and the kitchen just to survive, against a monopoly that can afford to think long term
(our paraphrase [WSJ14]). He said the same in 2014 about wages: a generous-pay rule is great, "but you need a
monopoly for this to work in practice." [AMA14]

## The lies people tell about markets

Monopolies pretend to be small fish in a big pond; non-monopolies pretend to be alone in a tiny one. In set
terms, he says, "a monopoly tells a lie where you describe your business as the union of these vastly
different markets and the non-monopolist describes it as the intersection." [YCL5]

| The lie | Who tells it | His example |
|---|---|---|
| Our market is tiny and we're alone in it (intersection) | Startups, non-monopolies | The only British restaurant in Palo Alto [WSJ14, YCL5] |
| Our market is huge and full of rivals (union) | Monopolies | Google as an ad company or a tech company [WSJ14, YCL5] |

**The intersection lie:** "The fatal temptation is to describe your market extremely narrowly so that you
dominate it by definition." [WSJ14] His line for it: "The something of somewhere is really mostly just the
nothing of nowhere" [YCL5]. A pitch that stacks buzzwords, or a film pitched as a mash-up of genres, is usually
just one more entry in a crowded category (our paraphrase of his examples [YCL5]).

**The union lie, with dated numbers.** As of May 2014 Google had "about 68% of the search market" [WSJ14] (about
66% in the lecture [YCL5]). Framed as an ad company it would hold about 3.4% of global advertising; framed as a
tech company, under a quarter of a percent of consumer tech (paraphrasing [WSJ14]).

## The Castro Street restaurants (2001)

The essay's story: in 2001 the PayPal team ate lunch among dozens of competing restaurants on Castro Street,
while "PayPal was then the only email-based payments company in the world." [WSJ14] It had fewer staff than the
restaurants combined and was worth far more than all of them (our paraphrase).

## The one question he asks

> "what is the actual market? So not what's the narrative of the market" [YCL5]

## Monopoly is not stasis

"Creative monopolists give customers more choices by adding entirely new categories of abundance to the
world." [WSJ14] And: "Monopoly is the condition of every successful business." [WSJ14]

> "All happy companies are different: Each one earns a monopoly by solving a unique problem. All failed
> companies are the same: They failed to escape competition." [WSJ14]

**Who captures the value, historically.** His examples [YCL5]: the Wright brothers flew the first plane and
"you don't make any money"; the railroads were valuable but mostly went bankrupt from competition; the textile
mills of 1780 to 1850 improved for decades and the gains were competed away (our paraphrase of the last two).

## How to apply it (our reading)

**The market reality check, step by step.** The steps are ours; the tests are his [YCL5, WSJ14].

1. **Write the pitch market** with every qualifier: *the only X for Y in Z*.
2. **Drop one qualifier at a time.** At each step, list who else serves that market and estimate its size.
3. **Name what customers substitute for you today,** including doing nothing and spreadsheets. That is usually
   the actual market.
4. **Write the union version:** if you were dominant and wanted to look small, what giant market would you say
   you're in?
5. **Estimate X and Y separately.** X is the yearly value you create for customers. Y is the share you can
   keep: your revenue divided by X.
6. **Decide:** real small market, fictional intersection, or too big to start.

**Worked numbers (fictional).** A startup pitches *AI-powered menu pricing for independent vegan restaurants in
Portland*: 140 restaurants, no direct rival. Drop *vegan*: 2,900 restaurants, two local rivals. Drop *Portland*:
a national market with a dozen menu-pricing tools. Drop *AI*: every point-of-sale vendor's reporting tab. The
substitute customers actually use is the POS report plus the owner's gut. So the real market is *pricing advice
for small restaurants*, and it's crowded. X: if the tool lifts margin by 1 point on $900k revenue, that's $9,000
a year per restaurant. Y: at $99 a month, the startup keeps about $1,200, roughly 13% of X. Y is reasonable;
the problem is that the market isn't really empty.

**Failure modes (our reading):**
- **Adjectives as moats.** If the category only exists because of qualifiers, you're in the intersection lie.
- **Big X, tiny Y.** Real value, no capture: good for the world, hard to fund.
- **Confusing "no competitors" with "no substitutes".** Substitutes are competition.
- **Describing the market for investors, not for yourself.** Write the honest version first.

**Limits:** his examples (airlines, Google, restaurants) are dated 2012 to 2014. Union and intersection are
framing tests, not a sizing method; the sizing steps above are ours.

**Use it now:** `templates/02-market-reality-check.md`.

**Checks to run:**
1. Write your market as an intersection (the only X for Y in Z). Now drop each qualifier. Who are you
   competing with at each step?
2. What is the actual market, in dollars, that customers already spend on solving this?
3. Are you building a big X with a tiny Y? What would let you capture more?
4. If you had a monopoly, how would you describe your market to hide it? Is that description closer to the
   truth than your pitch?
