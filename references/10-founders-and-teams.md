# 10 · Founders and teams

Citation IDs are in `SOURCES.md`. Most of this chapter comes from Blake Masters' class notes, labelled on each
quote. CS183-18 (Founder as victim, founder as god) was Thiel alone. In CS183-05, guests Stephen Cohen and Max
Levchin spoke; only lines the notes give to Thiel are used as his. Masters notes the influence of René Girard on
CS183-18.

## Founders are extreme

> "PayPal's founding team was six people. Four of them were born outside of the United States. Five of them were
> 23 or younger. Four of them built bombs when they were in high school." [CS183-18, from Blake Masters' class
> notes]

> "Perhaps the founder distribution is, however strangely, an inverted normal distribution. Both tails are
> extremely fat. Perhaps founders are complex combinations of, e.g., extreme insiders and extreme outsiders at
> the same time." [CS183-18, from Blake Masters' class notes]

What he looks for in people, in his own words: "You want people who are both really stubborn and really
open-minded. That's a little bit contradictory. You want people who are idiosyncratic and really different, but
then who can work well together in teams." [CWT15]

On Elon Musk when they first met: "Very smart, very charismatic, and incredibly driven -- a very rare
combination, since most people who have one of these traits learn to coast on the other two." [AMA14]

Experience is not the filter: "If you look at very successful companies, it's very common that the founders had
no prior experience at all." [CS183-10, from Blake Masters' class notes, Thiel speaking] And: "Elon didn't have
any experience in making rockets before he started SpaceX." [CS183-15, from Blake Masters' class notes]

## Founder as god, founder as victim

The notes trace the founder to the archaic king, worshipped and then sacrificed: "Kings became scapegoats who
had not yet been killed. Every king was a living god. Every god was a murdered king." [CS183-18, from Blake
Masters' class notes]

> "Push too hard and the poles reverse; you end up as an extreme outsider and it all goes to pot." [CS183-18,
> from Blake Masters' class notes]

The case studies in the notes: Bill Gates going from worship in the 1990s to the target of rivals and the DOJ;
Steve Jobs pushed out, then "12 crazy years from 1985 to '97" with conventional CEOs, then his return [CS183-18].
"Resurrections are possible. But you can only be resurrected after you die." [CS183-18]

## A startup is a monarchy, for a reason

> "A startup is basically structured as a monarchy." [CS183-18, from Blake Masters' class notes]

> "If you try to submit everything to voting processes when you're trying to do something new, you end up with
> bad, lowest common denominator type results." [CS183-18, from Blake Masters' class notes]

> "So the best arrangement is a quasi-mythological structure where you have a king-like founder who can do more
> than in a democratic ruler but who remains far from all-powerful." [CS183-18, from Blake Masters' class
> notes]

> "Monarchy involves going from 0 to 1. Democracy involves going from 1 to 99." [CS183-18, from Blake Masters'
> class notes]

## Protect the founding

**Extend it:** "In tech companies, foundings last as long as technological innovation continues." [CS183-18]
"The key is to encourage and achieve perpetual innovation. It is very important to avoid, or at least delay, the
shift to a horrible bureaucracy where no one can do anything and everyone is circumscribed." [CS183-18, from
Blake Masters' class notes]

**Have cofounders:** "The more singular and isolated the founder, the more dangerous the scapegoating
phenomenon." [CS183-18, from Blake Masters' class notes]

**Watch the inside:** the notes say "most fatal wounds come from internal, not external conflict." [CS183-18]

**The PayPal story, October 2000:** "The burn rate was $10 million/month. There were about 4.5 months of runway
left." [CS183-18] Thiel became interim CEO, a CEO search failed, and before the IPO the board wanted a COO: "I
was able to convince the board to make David Sacks COO, which was probably a good, safe move since David was
perceived to be crazier than I was." [CS183-18, from Blake Masters' class notes]

## Culture: closer to a cult than to consultants

> "To the extent you gravitate towards an extreme, you probably want to be closer to being a cult than being an
> army of consultants." [CS183-05, from Blake Masters' class notes]

> "A robust company culture is one in which people have something in common that distinguishes them quite
> sharply from rest of the world." [CS183-05, from Blake Masters' class notes]

**But surface disagreement:** "Everyone moving together in lockstep is a big red flag, not an ideal." [CS183-05,
from Blake Masters' class notes, Thiel speaking]

**PayPal's no-fire goal, and its limit:** "One early goal at PayPal was never to fire anybody." [CS183-05] "But
the flipside is that if you wait until it's obvious to everyone that someone should be fired, it's far too late."
[CS183-05, from Blake Masters' class notes]

**The founders set the tone:** "it is the founders of companies that set the cultural tone for so much that
happens in Silicon Valley." [AMA14]

## Hiring

**Hire people you know.** At PayPal, per the notes: "The founders agreed that PayPal could not afford to hire
sketchy people. So they just hired their friends instead." [CS183-02] On engineer referrals, after a guest
described them, Thiel: "That recruiting strategy has worked well in every company that I've been involved
with." [CS183-16, from Blake Masters' class notes]

**Answer the 20th-employee question:** "Why would the 20th engineer join your company when they could go to
Google instead and get more money and prestige?" [CS183-04, from Blake Masters' class notes] His answer: "The
first step is to avoid competing on purely financial terms, where you're likely to lose. You have to have that
compelling monopoly narrative that we discussed last week." [CS183-05, from Blake Masters' class notes, Thiel
speaking]

**Spend real time on it:** "between 25% and 33% of their time identifying and attracting talent" for founders
or one or two key senior people [CS183-09].

**Keep the team technical where the product is technical.** On cleantech: "Most cleantech companies in the last
decade have had shockingly non-technical teams and cultures." [CS183-14] "Salespeople and athletes are
important, but they shouldn't run things." [CS183-14, from Blake Masters' class notes]

**Keep it small, and in one place.** "North of 100 people in a company, employees don't all know each other.
Politics become important." [CS183-01] Investors "should and do seriously reconsider before investing in
companies that have more than one office." [CS183-01, from Blake Masters' class notes]

**Give each person one thing.** See chapter 09: PayPal redrew the org chart every three months so people weren't
fighting over the same role [CS183-12].

**Checks to run:**
1. What do your people have in common that sets them sharply apart from everyone else?
2. Why would your next great engineer join you instead of the most prestigious company hiring?
3. Are disagreements surfacing, or is everyone moving in lockstep?
4. Is any one person the single point of blame if things go wrong? Who shares the founding with them?
