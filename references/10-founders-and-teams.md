# 10 · Founders and teams

Citation IDs are in `SOURCES.md`. **Much of this chapter comes from Blake Masters' class notes,** labelled on
each quote and not a transcript. CS183-18 (Founder as victim, founder as god) was Thiel alone. In CS183-05,
guests Stephen Cohen and Max Levchin spoke; only lines the notes give to Thiel are used as his. His own later
words come from [CWT15], [AMA14], [HAM16], [FQA14] and [WBUR14].

## Founders are extreme

The notes describe PayPal's six founders: four born outside the US, five aged 23 or younger [CS183-18]. In his
own 2016 telling: "When we started, I was the only person over 23 years old." [HAM16] The notes' hypothesis is
that founders sit in both tails at once, "extreme insiders and extreme outsiders at the same time." [CS183-18,
from Blake Masters' class notes]

What he looks for, in his own words: "You want people who are both really stubborn and really open-minded."
[CWT15] And "people who are idiosyncratic and really different, but then who can work well together in teams."
[CWT15]

On Elon Musk when they first met: "Very smart, very charismatic, and incredibly driven -- a very rare
combination" [AMA14]. Experience is not the filter: the notes have Thiel saying the founders of very successful
companies often had no prior experience at all [CS183-10]. His own reason, 2014: "You needed to be naive enough
to think that new things could be done." [FQA14]

## Founder as god, founder as victim

The notes trace the founder to the archaic king, worshipped and then sacrificed, and warn: "Push too hard and
the poles reverse" [CS183-18, from Blake Masters' class notes]. Their case studies are Bill Gates in the 1990s and
Steve Jobs being pushed out and returning [CS183-18].

## A startup is a monarchy, for a reason

> "A startup is basically structured as a monarchy." [CS183-18, from Blake Masters' class notes]

The notes argue that putting everything to a vote produces lowest-common-denominator results, and that the best
arrangement is a founder with more power than an elected leader but far from all-powerful [CS183-18]. Their
summary: "Monarchy involves going from 0 to 1. Democracy involves going from 1 to 99." [CS183-18]

## Protect the founding

Per the notes: foundings last as long as innovation continues, so the job is to delay the shift to bureaucracy;
the more isolated the founder, the greater the risk of scapegoating; and most fatal wounds are internal
[CS183-18]. The PayPal story from October 2000 in the notes: a $10 million monthly burn and about 4.5 months of
runway [CS183-18].

## Culture: closer to a cult than to consultants

> "To the extent you gravitate towards an extreme, you probably want to be closer to being a cult than being an
> army of consultants." [CS183-05, from Blake Masters' class notes]

A strong culture, per the notes, is one where people share something that sets them "quite sharply from rest of
the world" [CS183-05]. But "Everyone moving together in lockstep is a big red flag, not an ideal." [CS183-05,
from Blake Masters' class notes, Thiel speaking]

His own later line on who sets culture: "it is the founders of companies that set the cultural tone for so much
that happens in Silicon Valley." [AMA14] And on mission: great businesses need "some transcendent sense of
mission" and it's "not as if it's just about making money." [WBUR14]

## The PayPal network, lightly

What the sources support: the class on culture was titled *The Mechanics of Mafia* [CS183-05], and the notes
credit PayPal's tight early culture to hiring friends who trusted each other [CS183-02, CS183-05]. In his own
words, his "PayPal colleague Elon Musk started both SpaceX and Tesla" [CWT15]. **Our reading:** the sources here
don't explain why so many PayPal alumni founded companies. The nearest thing is the culture they describe:
people who shared a lot and trusted each other, then went on to work together again.

## Hiring

**Hire people you know.** At PayPal, per the notes, the founders "just hired their friends instead." [CS183-02]

**Answer the 20th-employee question.** The notes ask why the 20th engineer would join you instead of Google, and
Thiel's answer is not to compete on money but to have a compelling monopoly story [CS183-04, CS183-05].

**Spend real time on it.** The notes say founders or one or two senior people should spend a quarter to a third
of their time finding talent [CS183-09].

**Keep the team technical where the product is technical.** On cleantech, the notes say: "Salespeople and
athletes are important, but they shouldn't run things." [CS183-14, from Blake Masters' class notes] His own
version: he likes ventures with committed founders, not part-time professors [AMA14].

**Keep it small, and in one place.** The notes say past about 100 people employees stop knowing each other, and
investors are wary of companies with more than one office [CS183-01].

## How to apply it (our reading)

1. **Write the one thing your team shares** that sets it apart from most people. If you can't, culture is unset.
2. **Run the 20th-engineer test:** write, in three sentences, why your next great engineer joins you over the
   most prestigious company hiring. No salary in the answer.
3. **Check for lockstep:** in the last month's decisions, how many had a recorded dissent? Zero is a flag.
4. **Check founder isolation:** who shares the blame if this fails? If one name, add a cofounder or a strong
   number two.
5. **Spend the time:** block a quarter of founder hours for recruiting during hiring pushes [CS183-09].

**Worked scenario (fictional).** A 14-person robotics startup loses three of its last five engineering offers
to big companies, all on salary. Their pitch was benefits and growth. They rewrite it around the one thing no big
company offers: owning a whole subsystem of the first robot that works in cold storage. The CEO moves from 5% to
25% of her week on recruiting. Next quarter they close four of six offers at the same salary band.

**Failure modes (our reading):**
- **A cult with no dissent.** Strong culture and lockstep are different things [CS183-05].
- **Hiring by résumé.** Experience is not his filter [CS183-10, FQA14].
- **The lone founder.** Isolation makes the scapegoat dynamic worse [CS183-18].
- **Competing on pay with a monopoly.** You'll lose.

**Limits:** the monarchy and scapegoat ideas are from the 2012 notes, written under Girard's influence, and are
an interpretation, not a management manual. The tests above are ours.

**Use it now:** `templates/05-founding-checklist.md` (team and culture sections).

**Checks to run:**
1. What do your people have in common that sets them sharply apart from everyone else?
2. Why would your next great engineer join you instead of the most prestigious company hiring?
3. Are disagreements surfacing, or is everyone moving in lockstep?
4. Is any one person the single point of blame if things go wrong? Who shares the founding with them?
