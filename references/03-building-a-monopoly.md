# 03 · Building a monopoly

Citation IDs are in `SOURCES.md`. `[CS183-nn]` quotes are **from Blake Masters' class notes** of the 2012
course: near-verbatim, not Thiel's own transcript. `[YCL5]` (2014) is a transcript of Thiel speaking.

## The recipe, in three steps

> "There are three steps to creating a truly valuable tech company. First, you want to find, create, or
> discover a new market. Second, you monopolize that market. Then you figure out how to expand that monopoly
> over time." [CS183-04, from Blake Masters' class notes]

## Start with a market so small it looks worthless

> "You start with a really small market and you take over the whole market and then over time you find ways to
> expand that market in concentric circles." [YCL5]

> "The thing that's always a big mistake is going after a giant market on day one because that's typically
> evidence that you somehow haven't defined categories correctly" [YCL5]

> "Maybe it's much better to find or make a small market, excel, and own it. And yet, the single business idea
> that you hear most often is: the bigger the market, the better. That is utterly, totally wrong." [CS183-03,
> from Blake Masters' class notes (Masters missed this class and wrote it up from classmates' notes)]

**His four stories** [YCL5]:

| Company | First market | What happened | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amazon | "you start with just a bookstore, we have all the books in the world" | Then "all sorts of different forms of e-commerce" | [YCL5] |
| eBay | "you start with Pez dispensers, you move on to Beanie Babies" | Then online auctions for all kinds of goods | [YCL5] |
| PayPal | "power sellers on Ebay, which was about twenty thousand people" (Dec 1999 to Jan 2000) | "twenty five, thirty percent market penetration in two or three months" | [YCL5] |
| Facebook | "ten thousand people at Harvard" | "zero to sixty percent market share in ten days" | [YCL5] |

On the PayPal power sellers: "we thought these were terrible customers to have, it's just people selling junk on
the internet, why in the world we want to be going after this market?" [YCL5]

**The expansion path**, in the class notes: "find a small target market, become the best in the world at
serving it, take over immediately adjacent markets, widen the aperture of what you're doing, and capture more
and more." [CS183-04, from Blake Masters' class notes]

**The inversion that fails:** "The best way to fail is to invert this recipe by starting big and shrinking.
Pets.com, Webvan, and Kozmo.com made this mistake." [CS183-04, from Blake Masters' class notes]

**Cleantech, 2005 to 2008:** every deck "started with where the energy market, where the market was measured in
hundreds and billions of trillions of dollars. And then once you're sort of like a minnow in a vast ocean, that
is not a good place to be." [YCL5]

> "You don't want to be the fourth online pet food company. You don't want to be the tenth solar panel company.
> You don't want to be the hundredth restaurant in Palo Alto." [YCL5]

**Too small is also a failure.** PayPal's first idea (beaming money between Palm Pilots): "No one else was doing
it, which was good. But no one really needed it done, which was bad." [CS183-04, from Blake Masters' class
notes] **Our reading:** the small market must be real people with a real, urgent need, not an empty niche.

## The four characteristics

> "For a company to own its market, it must have some combination of brand, scale cost advantages, network
> effects, or proprietary technology." [CS183-04, from Blake Masters' class notes]

### 1 · Proprietary technology: 10x on one dimension

> "My sort of crazy, somewhat arbitrary rule of thumb is you want to have a technology that's an order of
> magnitude better than the next best thing." [YCL5]

His examples [YCL5]: Amazon had "over ten times as many books". Checks on eBay took "seven to ten days to
clear, and PayPal could do it more than ten times as fast." Google's PageRank "was an order of magnitude better
than any other search engine." And: "if you just come with something totally new, it's just like an infinite
improvement."

> "So I think the technology needs to be designed to give you a massive delta over the next the next best
> thing." [YCL5]

The notes put it as "it's absolutely critical to have some decisive advantage over the next best service."
[CS183-03, from Blake Masters' class notes] At PayPal that meant fraud-fighting software and cheaper funding
sources [CS183-03].

### 2 · Network effects: hard to start

> "the thing that is very tricky about network effects is they're often very hard to get started and so you
> know everyone understands how valuable they are. There's always this incredibly tricky question: why is it
> valuable to the first person who's doing something." [YCL5]

The cautionary tale is Xanadu, the hypertext project begun in 1963: "Xanadu's problem was that you needed
everyone to adopt it at once for the network to work. They didn't, so it didn't." [CS183-02, from Blake
Masters' class notes] **The move:** design for the first user, inside the small market, before the network
exists.

### 3 · Economies of scale

> "Economies of scale, if you have something with very high fixed costs and very low marginal costs, that's
> typically a monopoly-like business." [YCL5]

Software is good at this "because the marginal cost of software is zero." [YCL5]

### 4 · Brand: real, but not enough alone

> "I never quite understand how branding works, so I never invest in companies where it's just about branding
> but it is, I think, a real phenomenon that creates real value." [YCL5]

**Google, scored by him:** network effects in the ad network, PageRank, economies of scale from storing the
web, and brand: "so Google has all four. Maybe the proprietary technology is somewhat weaker at this point but
definitely it had all four, and maybe three and a half out of four now." [YCL5]

## Speed of adoption protects a small market

> "even if you have a small market, if adoption rate is too slow, there will be enough time for other people to
> enter that market and compete with you." [YCL5]

## The fifth pattern: vertical integration

He names two broad categories where inventors made money over 250 years. One is "vertically integrated complex
monopolies" like Ford and Standard Oil, which need "a very complex coordination, you've got a lot of pieces to
fit together in just the right way" [YCL5]. On Tesla and SpaceX: "I don't think there was a single 10X
breakthrough on battery storage" but "what was really impressive was integrating all these pieces together"
[YCL5]. SpaceX "pulled in all subcontractors" [YCL5].

> "And so vertical integration I think is sort of a very under explored modality of technological progress that
> people would do well to look at more." [YCL5]

## Palantir, in his words

> "at Palantir we started with a focus on the intelligence community, which is a small submarket. We had a
> proprietary technology that used a very different approach where it was focused on the human computer
> synthesis, rather than the substitution" [YCL5]

## Frontiers beat disruption

> "Much better than to disrupt is to find a frontier and go for it." [CS183-04, from Blake Masters' class notes]

**Our reading:** "disrupt" points you at an incumbent, which is a competitor. A frontier has nobody on it yet.

**Checks to run:**
1. Who is your first market, by name and count? Can you reach more than half of them within a year?
2. On which one dimension are you 10x better than the next best thing? Measured how?
3. Why would the very first user join before anyone else has?
4. Which of the four (tech, network, scale, brand) will you have in five years that you don't have today?
