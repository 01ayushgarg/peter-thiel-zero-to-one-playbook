# 01 · Zero to one, and secrets

Citation IDs are in `SOURCES.md`: `[YCL5]` 2014 Stanford lecture · `[WSJ14]` WSJ essay · `[NPR14]` NPR
interview · `[CWT15]` Conversations with Tyler · `[AMA14]` Reddit AMA · `[NR11]` National Review essay. These are
Thiel's own words. `[CS183-nn]` are **Blake Masters' class notes** from the 2012 course: near-verbatim, not a
transcript, and labelled as notes wherever quoted.

## Zero to one vs one to n

> "It's much easier to do horizontal progress, which I describe as globalization, copying things that work
> going from one to n, versus vertical progress, technology, doing new things, going from zero to one." [CWT15]

> "And I think technology involves intensive, vertical, zero to one progress. Globalization involves horizontal,
> copying, one to end [sic: one to n] progress." [NPR14]

His examples of zero to one: "someone who builds a first airplane or first home computer or first smartphone
that works like the Apple iPhone" [NPR14].

From Blake Masters' class notes: "There's little doubt that going from 0 to 1 is qualitatively different, and
almost always harder, than copying something n times." [CS183-01]

## Every moment happens once

> "I believe that every moment in the history of business only happens once. And so the next Bill Gates will
> not be building an operating system company. The next Larry Page will not be building a search engine. The
> next Mark Zuckerberg won't be building a social network." [NPR14]

> "If you are copying these people, you are not learning from them." [YCL5]

**Our reading:** a playbook of what worked for someone else is a playbook for 1 to n. This repo gives you questions, not
answers to copy. He makes the same point about advice: "If I give you some general answer, and everybody could
follow it, then if everybody followed that answer, it would be the wrong thing to do." [CWT15]

## The contrarian question

> "There's sort of an interview question I like to ask people which is tell me something that is true that very
> few people agree with you on. The business version of this question is what great business is nobody
> building?" [NPR14]

Why it's hard, in his words [NPR14]: "One of them is it's always hard to come up with new truths that people
have not yet understood. Secondly, it also requires a bit of courage because you often have to go against
social convention in pursuing certain lines of business."

> "Everyone has things they believe to be true that other people won't agree with you on. But they're not things
> you want to say." [CWT15]

**His own answer** (2014): "Most people believe that capitalism and competition are synonyms, and I think they
are opposites." [AMA14] And in 2015: "I think the monopoly idea, that the goal of every successful business is to
have a monopoly, that's on the border of what I want to say. But the really good ideas are way more dangerous
than that." [CWT15]

**Where the question comes from**, in Blake Masters' class notes [CS183-01]: three questions, "First, what is
valuable? Second, what can I do? And third, what is nobody else doing?" Then: "The intellectual rephrasing of
these questions is: What important truth do very few people agree with you on?" and "The business version is:
What valuable company is nobody building?" The shape of a good answer, in the notes: "Most people believe in X.
But the truth is !X."

He gives a version for every role [CWT15]: "The contrarian business question is what are great businesses no
one has started, the contrarian investor question is what great investments does nobody like".

## Secrets: easy, hard, impossible

From Blake Masters' class notes on the "Secrets" class [CS183-11]:

> "Secrets are unpopular or unconventional truths." [CS183-11, from Blake Masters' class notes]

| Kind of truth | What it is | Worth your time? |
|---|---|---|
| Conventions | Easy truths everyone already knows | No edge |
| Secrets | Hard but findable | Yes: "Secrets exist. And finding them is neither easy nor impossible—just hard." [CS183-11] |
| Mysteries | Impossible for now: "These are mysteries, not secrets." [CS183-11] | Not as a business |

> "Probably the biggest secret—bigger than monopoly/competition, power law, or distribution—is that there are
> many important secrets left." [CS183-11, from Blake Masters' class notes]

**Why people stopped looking.** The notes list four drivers of disbelief in secrets: incrementalism, risk
aversion, complacency and egalitarianism [CS183-11]. On risk aversion: "If your goal is to never
make mistake in your life, you should definitely never think about secrets." [CS183-11, from Blake Masters'
class notes]

**Two places to look:**

> "There are secrets of nature and then there are secrets about people." [CS183-11, from Blake Masters' class
> notes]

The questions to ask, from the notes: "What secrets is nature not telling you? What secrets are people not
telling you?" And for the human kind: "What kinds of things are we allowed to talk about? Are there areas that
people can't look into? What is explicitly forbidden? What is implicitly off-limits or taboo?" [CS183-11]

His examples in the notes: Tesla started from the human secret that cleantech was fashion ("You could start
Tesla, which is exactly what Elon Musk did." [CS183-11]); PayPal's fraud problem was a secret banks kept,
because they "don't like to come out and say" how much is stolen from them [CS183-11].

## What to do with a secret

> "If you don't tell anyone, you'll keep the secret safe. But no one will work with you." [CS183-11, from
> Blake Masters' class notes]

The PayPal case in the notes (summer 1999): linking money to email was a big but easy-to-see secret, so the
team told people widely and moved fast [CS183-11]. **The rule, in the notes:** "The bigger the secret and the
likelier it is that you alone have it, the more time you have to execute." [CS183-11]

**Our reading:** tell your cofounders and the people you need. Don't pitch the secret to the world before you
can act on it.

## Secrets beat luck

> "Belief in secrets is an effective truth. Belief in luck is an ineffective truth; it will stop you from
> actually doing things." [CS183-13, from Blake Masters' class notes]

## A counterfactual sense of mission

> "Always having a counterfactual sense of mission is important. If we weren't doing this, nobody else in the
> world would be doing this." [CWT15]

> "It's good to be passionate about something that you are good at, that other people are not doing. If you get
> those three things lined up, that's a very good start." [CWT15]

## Big trends are not secrets

Asked about virtual reality in 2014: "Massive trend, but I think the valuable 0-1 companies will identify a more
concrete area of focus within this large trend." [AMA14] On AI in 2015: "It feels like a bit of an extreme
consensus that AI is just around the corner, it's about to happen." [CWT15] **Our reading:** if everyone agrees,
it's a convention, not a secret. Look for the specific, unpopular piece inside it.

## Where his urgency comes from

His wider thesis is that progress slowed outside computers: "there has been a lot of progress in the world of
bits and not so much in the world of atoms." [NPR14] Zero to one is how you get out of that.
