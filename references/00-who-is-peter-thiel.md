# 00 · Who Peter Thiel is, for this playbook

This playbook is about one thing: how Peter Thiel says he thinks about startups, business strategy and
technology. It doesn't cover his politics, his political donations or his personal life.

What the sources here cover:
- **PayPal:** he co-founded it [WSJ14, NPR14]. The class notes cover its early years in detail: the 1999 switch
  to email payments, viral growth, the X.com merger, and the sale to eBay for $1.5bn [CS183-02, CS183-07,
  CS183-09, CS183-12]. In his own later telling, the first users were the people who worked at the company, and
  the founding goal was far bigger than a payments product [HAM16].
- **Palantir:** he co-founded it [WSJ14, NPR14]. It started with "a focus on the intelligence community, which
  is a small submarket" [YCL5].
- **Facebook:** NPR's introduction describes him as its first outside investor [NPR14], and the WSJ bio line says
  he "made the first outside investment in Facebook." [WSJ14] His own verdict on the follow-on round he skipped is
  in chapter 07 [AMA14].
- **Founders Fund:** his venture firm [NPR14, YCL5]. The class notes link the firm's name to the idea that
  foundings are crucial [CS183-06].
- **Stanford CS183 (spring 2012):** a course on startups he taught at Stanford. Student Blake Masters published
  near-verbatim notes of each class [CS183-01 to CS183-19]. The 2014 book *Zero to One*, written with Masters,
  grew out of the class [WSJ14, NPR14]. In his words, the book's goal "was to pack this three-month class into a
  very disciplined 200 pages." [AMA14]
- **Stanford CS183B (fall 2014):** his guest lecture "Competition is for Losers" in Sam Altman's How to Start a
  Startup course [YCL5].
- **The Thiel Fellowship:** a fellowship for young people who want to build something instead of following the
  usual track. He says he started it because schools "tend to breed a kind of process-oriented conformity"
  [FQA14]. Chapter 13 covers the non-political parts.

## Two kinds of source, labelled differently

- **His own words:** the 2014 lecture transcript [YCL5], his WSJ essay [WSJ14], interviews [NPR14, WBUR14,
  CWT15, CWT24, FQA14], the 2012 Fortune debate with Eric Schmidt [FS12], his 2016 Hamilton College commencement
  speech [HAM16], his Reddit answers [AMA14] and his essays [NR11, NYT15].
- **Class notes:** Blake Masters' notes of the 2012 course. Masters says they are not a transcript. Every quote
  from them is labelled as from Blake Masters' class notes. Some classes had guest speakers; their views are
  named as theirs. Class 8 was given by Bruce Gibney of Founders Fund, not Thiel.

**Our reading** of the balance: the 2012 notes are the richest source on distribution, the founding, the power
law and teams. His own later words cover the same ground more thinly. Where a later first-party line exists, the
chapters use it; where it doesn't, the chapter says it rests on the notes.

## The thesis behind everything

His one obsession on the business side, in the 2014 lecture: "you always want to aim for monopoly and you want
to always avoid competition." [YCL5]

And the wider backdrop, from his 2011 essay: "The speedup in information technology contrasts dramatically with
the slowdown everywhere else." [NR11] Chapter 12 covers that thesis.

## Read him with his own caveats

> "If I give you some general answer, and everybody could follow it, then if everybody followed that answer, it
> would be the wrong thing to do." [CWT15]

He also knows the monopoly advice can discourage people who don't have a monopoly idea, and his answer is
"we should be starting more good businesses and fewer bad ones." [CWT15]

He worries about turning ideas into formulas: "I worry that the only way you can scale things is by somehow
automating them, mechanizing them, turning them into more of a cookie cutter-type process." [CWT24]

**Our reading:** this repo is a set of questions to think with, not a formula to copy. Where the honest answer is
that you maybe shouldn't start this company, the skill says so.

## How to use the chapters (our suggestion)

1. **Looking for an idea:** 01 (secrets), then 12 (frontiers) and 13 (tracks).
2. **Testing an idea:** 02 (the actual market), 03 (monopoly), 04 (durability).
3. **Growth has stalled:** 05 (distribution), then 07 (one channel, one revenue line).
4. **Setting up or raising:** 06 (founding), 10 (teams).
5. **Feeling stuck in a race:** 09 (mimesis), 08 (plans).
6. **Before any big decision:** 11 (the question bank).

**Limits:** none of these sources is a complete operating manual. He gives questions and examples, not
procedures. The procedures in each chapter are ours, marked as our reading, and should be judged on whether
they help you think, not on his authority.
