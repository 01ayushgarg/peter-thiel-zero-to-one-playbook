# 08 · Definite optimism: have a plan

Citation IDs are in `SOURCES.md`. The core of this chapter is the 2012 class "You Are Not A Lottery Ticket"
[CS183-13], which Thiel taught alone. All `[CS183-nn]` quotes are **from Blake Masters' class notes**, not a
transcript. His own words come from [NPR14], [WBUR14], [CWT15], [AMA14], [YCL5], [HAM16] and [NR11].

## You are not a lottery ticket

The notes frame the biggest philosophical question about startups as how much luck is involved, and argue that
"Skill probably plays a much greater role than people typically think." [CS183-13, from Blake Masters' class
notes] The evidence offered is repeat founders such as Jobs, Dorsey and Musk [CS183-13].

> "You aren't a lottery ticket, so you shouldn't think of failure as just 1 of n times that you're going to
> start a company." [CS183-01, from Blake Masters' class notes]

## The four quadrants

The notes cross two questions: is the future better or worse, and can you know how? [CS183-13]

| | Definite (determinate) | Indefinite (indeterminate) |
|---|---|---|
| **Optimistic** | Engineering and art | Law and finance |
| **Pessimistic** | Wartime rationing | Insurance |

(Cell labels as written in the class notes [CS183-13].)

If the future is indeterminate you diversify; if it's determinate, the notes say, "There is just one thing—the
best thing—that you should do." [CS183-13, from Blake Masters' class notes] His own example of definite optimism
in 2014: "The dual Elon Musk empire of Tesla and SpaceX." [AMA14]

## Extreme optimism and pessimism both lead to nothing

> "extreme pessimism tells you there's no point in doing anything. Extreme optimism tells you there is no need to
> do anything. They converge on doing nothing." [CWT15]

On radio in 2014 he called both "mindsets that I think lead to inaction." [NPR14] Later that month: "You will
have low expectations. And low expectations will be met." [WBUR14] And the future "depends on human agency and
the desire to build it" [WBUR14]. His 2011 essay: "Progress is neither automatic nor mechanistic; it is rare."
[NR11]

## Plans beat iteration

The notes list what indefinite thinking looks like inside startups: A/B testing as strategy, iteration with no
destination, short horizons [CS183-13]. "Darwinism takes billions of years to work reasonably well. Startups
don't have that time." [CS183-13, from Blake Masters' class notes] Apple is the counterexample, with deliberate
design at every level [CS183-13].

His own picture of a definite plan is PayPal's: "Very definite, very big plans." The goal was to replace the
dollar with a new digital currency; that failed, but the company that came out of it succeeded [HAM16].

**The career version.** He looks back on his plan to become a lawyer as "less like a plan for the future and
more like an alibi for the present." [HAM16] Chapter 13 picks this up.

## His skepticism of the lean startup (2014)

Asked directly in the 2014 lecture: "I'm personally quite skeptical of all the lean startup methodology." [YCL5]
His reasons, paraphrased: the great companies made a quantum improvement rather than iterating toward one, and
taking a long time to find out what people want can mean missing the boat [YCL5]. He also argues that a
low-risk track like law school can carry a high risk of not doing something meaningful [YCL5].

**Our reading:** this is a disagreement with a popular method, and he presents it as his view. Use it as a check
on whether iteration has replaced a plan, not as a rule against talking to customers. Chapter 05 is clear that
distribution still has to be designed.

## Plans and selling the company

Per the notes, a company with a good plan is undervalued in a world that doesn't believe in plans, and such
companies "typically do not sell." [CS183-13, from Blake Masters' class notes] The notes say PayPal sold in 2002
because it had run out of ideas [CS183-13].

> "Your plans can change, but if you don't have them, you're just floating with the current." [CS183-13, from
> Blake Masters' class notes]

## How to apply it (our reading)

**A one-page definite plan.** The format is ours, built on his PayPal example [HAM16] and the notes [CS183-13].

1. **The end state in ten years,** in one concrete sentence: who the customers are and what you are to them.
2. **Three steps,** each of which funds or unlocks the next.
3. **The secret** the plan rests on (chapter 01).
4. **What you'll say no to** to stay on it.
5. **Which questions you will settle by testing** (pricing, copy, onboarding) and which you won't (the market,
   the product's core).
6. **A review date.** Plans can change; drifting is the failure.

**Worked scenario (fictional).** A team building tools for small solar installers has 40 experiments queued and
no end state. Their plan: *in ten years, the operating system for 30,000 US installers.* Step 1: permit
paperwork for California installers (owned in two years). Step 2: financing offers inside the same workflow
(the revenue engine). Step 3: supplier ordering. Of the 40 experiments, 31 don't serve step 1 and are dropped.
The 9 left are tests of copy and pricing, which is where testing belongs.

**Failure modes (our reading):**
- **A vision with no steps.** An end state with nothing about how to get there.
- **Steps with no end state.** A roadmap of experiments.
- **A plan nobody can change.** Definite isn't rigid; set the review date.
- **Mistaking optimism for a plan.** His warning about expecting the future to arrive by itself [CWT15].

**Limits:** the four quadrants and most planning lines are from the 2012 notes. His lean-startup view is one
person's opinion, stated as such.

**Use it now:** `templates/09-definite-plan.md`, `templates/06-ten-year-test.md`.

**Checks to run:**
1. Can you write the company's plan for the next ten years in one page? Does it name a specific end state?
2. Which decisions are you leaving to A/B tests that are really strategy decisions?
3. Is your roadmap a plan, or a list of experiments with no destination?
4. If your plan is good, would you sell the company today? Why not?
