# 08 · Definite optimism: have a plan

Citation IDs are in `SOURCES.md`. The core of this chapter is the 2012 class "You Are Not A Lottery Ticket"
[CS183-13], which Thiel taught alone. All `[CS183-nn]` quotes are **from Blake Masters' class notes**, not a
transcript.

## You are not a lottery ticket

> "The biggest philosophical question underlying startups is how much luck is involved when they succeed."
> [CS183-13, from Blake Masters' class notes]

> "But people do tend to be extremely biased towards the luck side of things. Skill probably plays a much
> greater role than people typically think." [CS183-13, from Blake Masters' class notes]

His evidence in the notes is repeat founders: "But it seems very odd to argue that Jobs, Dorsey, or Musk just
got lucky." [CS183-13]

> "You aren't a lottery ticket, so you shouldn't think of failure as just 1 of n times that you're going to
> start a company." [CS183-01, from Blake Masters' class notes]

## The four quadrants

The notes cross two questions: is the future better or worse (optimism or pessimism), and can you know how
(definite or indefinite)? [CS183-13]

| | Definite (determinate) | Indefinite (indeterminate) |
|---|---|---|
| **Optimistic** | Engineering and art | Law and finance |
| **Pessimistic** | Wartime rationing | Insurance |

(Cell labels exactly as written in the class notes [CS183-13].)

> "If you believe that the future is fundamentally indeterminate, you would stress diversification."
> [CS183-13, from Blake Masters' class notes]

> "If the future is determinate, it makes much more sense to have firm convictions. You won't join tons of
> different clubs or do every single activity. There is just one thing—the best thing—that you should do."
> [CS183-13, from Blake Masters' class notes]

> "In a future of definite optimism, you get underwater cities and cities in space. In a world of indefinite
> optimism, you get finance." [CS183-13, from Blake Masters' class notes]

His own example of definite optimism, asked in 2014: "The dual Elon Musk empire of Tesla and SpaceX." [AMA14]

## Extreme optimism and pessimism both lead to nothing

> "If you're extremely optimistic, then the future will take care of itself. If you're extremely pessimistic,
> then there's nothing they can do. And so they both are mindsets that I think lead to inaction." [NPR14]

> "extreme pessimism tells you there's no point in doing anything. Extreme optimism tells you there is no need to
> do anything. They converge on doing nothing." [CWT15]

> "I disagree with the vision of the future where all you have to do is sit back, eat popcorn, and watch the
> movie of the future unfold." [CWT15]

> "I always believe in human agency and so I think it matters a great deal whether people end it or not."
> [CWT15]

> "Progress is neither automatic nor mechanistic; it is rare." [NR11]

## Plans beat iteration

What indefinite thinking looks like inside startups, per the notes [CS183-13]: "Darwinistic A/B testing",
iterative processes, machine learning, "No thinking about the future" and "Short time horizons".

> "Darwinism takes billions of years to work reasonably well. Startups don't have that time." [CS183-13, from
> Blake Masters' class notes]

> "But definite plans tend to be underrated in today's startup culture." [CS183-13, from Blake Masters' class
> notes]

Apple is the counterexample in the notes: "Apple is absolute antithesis of finance. It does deliberate design on
every level." [CS183-13] Great design "seems to work better and faster than Darwinistic A/B testing or
iteratively searching through an incredibly large search space." [CS183-13]

> "Winning without a plan is hitting the jackpot, and most people do not hit the jackpot." [CS183-14, from Blake
> Masters' class notes]

> "A good intermediate lesson in chess is that even a bad plan is better than no plan at all." [CS183-12, from
> Blake Masters' class notes, Thiel speaking]

The 1990s version: "There were lots of companies in the '90s that had launch parties but no landing parties."
[CS183-13, from Blake Masters' class notes]

## His skepticism of the lean startup (2014)

Asked directly in the 2014 lecture:

> "I'm personally quite skeptical of all the lean startup methodology. I think the really great companies did
> something that was somewhat more of a quantum improvement that really differentiated them from everybody
> else." [YCL5]

> "I do think we're way too focused on iteration as a modality and not enough on trying to have a virtual ESP
> link with the public and figuring it out ourselves." [YCL5]

On risk: "If you're going to take enough time to figure out what people want, you often will have missed the
boat by then." [YCL5] And the risk people miss: "You could say that a track in law school is a low risk track
from one perspective, but it may still be a very high risk track in the sense that maybe you have a high risk of
not doing something meaningful with your life." [YCL5]

**Our reading:** this is a disagreement with a popular method, and he presents it as his view. Use it as a check
on whether iteration has replaced a plan, not as a rule against talking to customers. Chapter 05 is clear that
distribution still has to be designed.

## Plans and selling the company

> "Any company with a good secret plan will always be undervalued in a world where nobody believes in secrets and
> nobody believes in plans." [CS183-13, from Blake Masters' class notes]

> "Related to this is the observation that companies with really good plans typically do not sell." [CS183-13,
> from Blake Masters' class notes] The notes say PayPal sold in 2002 because "it had run out of them" (ideas)
> [CS183-13].

## For your career

> "Young people today tend to be indeterminately optimistic. They iterate, one resume line at a time."
> [CS183-13, from Blake Masters' class notes]

> "Your plans can change, but if you don't have them, you're just floating with the current." [CS183-13, from
> Blake Masters' class notes]

> "There is a mini singularity whenever you start a company or make a key life decision." [CS183-19, from Blake
> Masters' class notes, Thiel's closing remarks]

**Use it now:** `templates/06-ten-year-test.md` (write the plan), `templates/01-secret-and-contrarian-truth.md`
(the secret it rests on).

**Checks to run:**
1. Can you write the company's plan for the next ten years in one page? Does it name a specific end state?
2. Which decisions are you leaving to A/B tests that are really strategy decisions?
3. Is your roadmap a plan, or a list of experiments with no destination?
4. If your plan is good, would you sell the company today? Why not?
