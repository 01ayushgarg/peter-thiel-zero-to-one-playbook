# 13 · Tracks, credentials and the Fellowship

Citation IDs are in `SOURCES.md`. `[HAM16]`, `[FQA14]`, `[NPR14]`, `[FS12]`, `[AMA14]`, `[CWT15]`, `[YCL5]` are
Thiel's own words. **Scope:** this chapter covers his views on career tracks, credentials and the Thiel
Fellowship as they bear on founders. Education policy and politics are out of scope.

## The track

His 2016 commencement speech is the fullest version. He had been "competitively tracked from middle school to
high school to college" [HAM16], and law school meant more of the same tests. The big New York firm was a place
everyone wanted to enter and everyone wanted to leave; he left after seven months and three days [HAM16].

The turning point was a loss: he missed a Supreme Court clerkship, "the absolute last stage of the competition"
[HAM16]. A decade later a friend asked whether he was glad, because without that loss he "never would have left
the track laid down since middle school" [HAM16].

> "Looking back at my ambition to become a lawyer, it looks less like a plan for the future and more like an
> alibi for the present." [HAM16]

The 2014 Q&A tells the same story more briefly, and draws the business lesson: things can be valuable "yet
ignored by most people, simply because they are not found on any conventional track." [FQA14]

## Why tracks pull founders into competition

In the same Q&A he says entrepreneurs face more uncertainty than professionals, so they reach for a guide, often
the latest moves of their competitors, and end up in "incremental battles with each other" (our paraphrase with
his phrase [FQA14]). The 2014 lecture adds the risk people miss: a safe-looking track like law school can carry a
high risk of not doing something meaningful [YCL5].

## Credentials

**College.** On radio in 2014: "I probably would still go to college, but I would think a lot harder about why I
went." [NPR14] He worried that education had become a substitute for thinking about the future [NPR14].

**The cost argument (2012).** In the Fortune debate he called education a bubble: "If there's anything in our
society that's a bubble, it is education." [FS12] His evidence, paraphrased: costs up roughly threefold after
inflation since 1980, with no clear rise in quality, and a shrinking gap in returns since 2000 [FS12]. These are
his 2012 figures, not checked here.

**MBAs.** He doesn't ban them; his concern is temperament and herd behaviour (chapter 09, [AMA14]).

## The Fellowship

What the sources say:
- **Why he started it:** schools, he says, breed a process-oriented conformity, so he started a fellowship for
  young people who want to learn by getting something done in the real world [FQA14].
- **The same reason as the class:** he taught at Stanford to tell students they don't have to accept the paths
  laid down by their schooling or their competitors [FQA14].
- **Results he claimed in 2014:** "the 83 fellows have collectively raised $63 million" [AMA14], and almost all
  of them, he says, learned far more than they would have in college [AMA14]. These are his own figures as of
  September 2014.
- **Who he looks for:** the same Zen-like opposites as in founders, stubborn and open-minded at once [CWT15].

## Inexperience as an edge

His PayPal lesson: if he had known how hard payments were, he would never have started it, and young people have
an advantage because they haven't yet been "brainwashed into thinking that current methods are inevitable."
[FQA14] The 2016 advice to graduates: "So do not squander your ignorance." [HAM16]

## Live as if forever

He ends the 2016 speech by flipping a cliché: live each day as if you will live forever, because the
consequences of your choices compound [HAM16]. He applies it to people: "you'll get the best returns in life from
investing your time in building durable friendships and long-lasting relationships." [HAM16] **Our reading:**
it's chapter 04's durability argument applied to a career.

## How to apply it: a track audit (our reading)

1. **List the last five big choices** you made (school, job, raise, hire, market).
2. **For each, write why.** Mark it *track* if the honest reason is that it was the next competition, the
   prestigious option, or what peers did. Mark it *chosen* if you can name what it was for.
3. **Count the tracks.** Three or more of five means your plan may be an alibi [HAM16].
4. **For the next big choice,** write the plan it serves (chapter 08) before you look at what peers are doing.
5. **Name the inexperience edge:** what do you not know about your field that might make you try something its
   veterans wouldn't?

**Worked scenario (fictional).** A second-year consultant plans an MBA, then a product role at a big company,
"then a startup". Audit: MBA (track), big-company role (track), startup (no plan). She replaces the sequence with
six months building a prototype for the freight-billing problem she saw at clients, with a written test: 10 paying
pilot customers by month six, or she applies for the MBA next cycle. The option stays; the alibi goes.

**Failure modes (our reading):**
- **Dropping out as a new track.** Leaving school because founders are supposed to is still mimesis.
- **Credential panic.** A degree isn't wrong; going without thinking about why is his point [NPR14].
- **Naivety as an excuse.** Inexperience helps with seeing new things, not with skipping hard work.

**Limits:** the education-cost figures are his 2012 claims; the Fellowship results are his 2014 claims. Policy
arguments about education are out of scope. The audit is ours.

**Checks to run:**
1. Is your current plan a plan for the future, or an alibi for the present?
2. Which of your last five choices were the next competition rather than a decision?
3. What do you know because you're new that the veterans in your field have stopped seeing?
4. Which relationships in your work are you treating as if they'll last forever?
