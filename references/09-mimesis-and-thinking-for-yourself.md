# 09 · Mimesis: why we compete, and how to stop

Citation IDs are in `SOURCES.md`. `[YCL5]`, `[CWT15]`, `[AMA14]`, `[CWT24]` are Thiel's own words. `[CS183-nn]`
quotes are **from Blake Masters' class notes**, not a transcript. Reid Hoffman was a guest in CS183-12; his views
are named as his.

## The psychological blind spot

> "I think it's more than just an intellectual blind spot, but also a psychological blind spot, where we find
> ourselves very attracted to competition and in one form or another we find it reassuring if other people do
> things." [YCL5]

> "The word ape, already in the time of Shakespeare, meant both primate and imitate and that is something about
> human nature, it's deeply imitative, ape like, sheep like and this is very problematic thing that we need to
> always think through and try to overcome." [YCL5]

## Competition as validation

> "It's not that there is wisdom of crowds, it's not that lots of people trying to do something is the best
> proof of that being valuable. I think it's when lots of people are trying to do something, that is often proof
> of insanity." [YCL5]

His number: "There are twenty thousand people a year who move to Los Angeles to become movie stars, about twenty
of them make it." [YCL5]

From the 2012 notes: "But too often in the race to compete, we learn to confuse what is hard with what is
valuable." [CS183-04] And the line about Rhodes Scholars: "they all had a great future in their past."
[CS183-04, from Blake Masters' class notes]

## The Kissinger line

He cites "the classic Henry Kissinger line describing his fellow faculty at Harvard" [YCL5]:

> "The battles were so ferocious because the stakes were so small" [YCL5]

Why it happens: "When it's been really hard to differentiate yourself from other people, when the differences
are, when the objectives differences really are small, you have to compete ferociously to maintain a difference
of one sort or another." [YCL5]

## The law-firm story

In his own words, twice. The 2014 lecture: his eighth-grade yearbook predicted Stanford, he went to Stanford Law
School and "ended up at a big law firm in New York where from the outside everybody wanted to get in and on the
inside everybody wanted to leave" and "I left after seven months and three days." [YCL5]

> "Other people down the hall told me, it's really reassuring to see you leave, Peter, I had no idea that it was
> possible to escape from Alcatraz, which of course all you had do was go out the front door and not come back."
> [YCL5]

> "But so much of people's identities got wrapped up in winning these competitions that they somehow lost sight
> of what was important, what was valuable." [YCL5]

The 2015 version adds what he learned: "I learned that I was incredibly prone to this problem of social
convention." [CWT15]

> "Don't always go through the tiny little door that everyone's trying to rush through, maybe go around the
> corner and go through the vast gate that nobody is taking." [YCL5]

## Nobody is immune

> "We always think of advertising as this thing that works on other people, for all the stupid people who follow
> ads on TV, but they obviously work to some extent and they work to the disturbing extent on all of us and it's
> something we must work to overcome." [YCL5]

> "I think often the smarter people are more prone to trendy, fashionable thinking because they can pick up on
> things, they can pick up on cues more easily, and so they're even more trapped by it than people of average
> ability." [CWT15]

On business school cohorts, he says the largest group "systematically ends up doing the wrong thing, they try to
catch the last wave" [YCL5], and he explains PayPal's wariness of MBAs: "most MBA's tend to be high
extrovert/low conviction people -- a combination that in my experience leads towards extremely herd-like
thinking and behavior" [AMA14]. The pressure is mostly from peers: "In practice, the greater problem is
horizontal, runaway peer pressure." [AMA14]

## Why some founders escape it

> "many of the more successful entrepreneurs seem to be suffering from a mild form of Asperger's where it's like
> you're missing the imitation, socialization gene." [CWT15]

> "We need to ask, what is it about our society where those of us who do not suffer from Asperger's are at some
> massive disadvantage because we will be talked out of our interesting, original, creative ideas before they
> are even fully formed?" [CWT15]

## Rivals become alike (Girard and Shakespeare)

From the 2012 class War and Peace, in Blake Masters' notes: "A working theory is thus that you must choose your
enemies well, since you'll soon become just like them." [CS183-12] People fight not because they differ (the
notes call this the Marx view) but because they're alike, the Shakespeare view: "In the great majority of
cases, it's straight Shakespeare. People become obsessed with their competitors." [CS183-12]

He put the same idea in his own words in 2024: "There's something in Shakespeare that's sort of
proto-Girardian or very mimetic, where people have conflicts when they . . . The conflicts are the most intense
when they don't differ at all." [CWT24]

**The stories in the notes** [CS183-12]:
- **Microsoft vs Google:** "In a way, they were destined to war with each other from day one because they are
  so alike." Then: "The irony is that Apple just came along and overtook them all."
- **Card readers:** after Square, rivals shipped readers in other shapes: "But they tried to one-up Square; 3
  sides, after all, was simpler than 4." He told the same joke in 2014: "one is a triangle, one is a square.
  Maybe at one point the apes run out of shapes" [YCL5].
- **X.com vs PayPal (1999 to 2000):** "X.com launched a feature-for-feature matching product, right down to the
  identical cash bonus and referral structure." The two companies then merged: "We agreed to a 50-50 merger in
  early March." [CS183-12] His own AMA: "It was kind of scary to be competiting [sic] against his startup in Palo
  Alto in Dec 1999-Mar 2000." [AMA14]

> "If you do have to fight a war, you must use overwhelming force and end it quickly." [CS183-12, from Blake
> Masters' class notes]

> "Focusing on your enemy is almost always the wrong thing to do." [CS183-12, from Blake Masters' class notes]

## Inside the company

> "Most companies are killed by internal infighting, even though it may not seem like it." [CS183-12, from
> Blake Masters' class notes]

Same cause: people fight "because they both want to do the same thing." [CS183-12] PayPal's fix: "PayPal solved
this problem by completely redrawing the org chart every three months." [CS183-12, from Blake Masters' class
notes] **Our reading:** give each person a distinct job, so no two people are competing for the same thing.

## Social, or seen as good?

He says a sense of mission is critical, but the word social is ambiguous: "it can mean either (1) good for
society, or (2) seen as good by society." [AMA14]

"In the second meaning, it leads to me-too copycat companies." [AMA14] The 2012 notes ask: "Is it actually good
for society? Or is it simply approved of by society?" [CS183-14]

## Ways out

> "Then I think there is another modality where you just become aware of how conventional our conventions really
> are, and then that becomes sort of an indirect route of trying to start thinking for yourself." [CWT15]

> "I do think this idea of what's unfashionable is very important as an initial anchor." [CWT15]

> "I would always be long substance, short status." [CWT15]

Contrarian for its own sake fails too: "But if the herd isn't thinking at all, being contrarian—doing the
opposite of the herd—is just as random and useless." [CS183-02, from Blake Masters' class notes]

And his honest caveat: "My first starting point, which is only going to be maybe ten percent of the way, is to
never underestimate how big a problem it is." [YCL5]
