# 09 · Mimesis: why we compete, and how to stop

Citation IDs are in `SOURCES.md`. `[YCL5]`, `[CWT15]`, `[AMA14]`, `[CWT24]`, `[HAM16]`, `[FQA14]` are Thiel's own
words. `[CS183-nn]` quotes are **from Blake Masters' class notes**, not a transcript. Reid Hoffman was a guest in
CS183-12; his views are named as his.

## The psychological blind spot

He calls our attraction to competition a psychological blind spot, not just an intellectual one: "we find it
reassuring if other people do things." [YCL5] He reaches for the old double meaning of a word: "The word ape,
already in the time of Shakespeare, meant both primate and imitate" [YCL5].

## Competition as validation

Many people chasing the same thing isn't proof of value: "when lots of people are trying to do something, that
is often proof of insanity." [YCL5] His illustration: each year thousands move to Los Angeles to become movie
stars and only a handful make it [YCL5].

The 2012 notes have the same warning: "we learn to confuse what is hard with what is valuable." [CS183-04, from
Blake Masters' class notes]

He cites Henry Kissinger on Harvard faculty politics: "The battles were so ferocious because the stakes were so
small" [YCL5]. His explanation, paraphrased: when real differences are small, people fight harder to keep any
difference at all [YCL5].

## The law-firm story

He tells it in his own words several times. The 2016 version: a big New York law firm where "From the outside,
everybody wanted to get in; and from the inside, everybody wanted to get out." [HAM16] He left after seven months
and three days; a colleague said he hadn't known it was possible to escape from Alcatraz [HAM16]. The reason
people stayed: "so much of their identity was wrapped up in having won the competitions to get there in the first
place." [HAM16]

In 2015 he put the lesson on himself: "I learned that I was incredibly prone to this problem of social
convention." [CWT15] In 2014: "Don't always go through the tiny little door that everyone's trying to rush
through" [YCL5].

## Nobody is immune

Advertising, he says, works "to the disturbing extent on all of us" [YCL5]. And smart people aren't safer: "I
think often the smarter people are more prone to trendy, fashionable thinking" [CWT15], because they pick up
social cues faster.

On PayPal's wariness of MBAs, in his own words: most "tend to be high extrovert/low conviction people" which leads
"towards extremely herd-like thinking and behavior" [AMA14]. The bigger pressure, he says, is from peers:
"horizontal, runaway peer pressure." [AMA14]

**On founders who resist the herd.** In 2015 he speculated that many successful entrepreneurs seem less
sensitive to social imitation than most people, and turned it into a question about society: why are the rest of
us talked out of original ideas so early? [CWT15] **Context:** he used a clinical label loosely in that remark.
We paraphrase it and don't treat it as a claim about any condition or as a hiring test.

## Rivals become alike (Girard and Shakespeare)

From the 2012 class War and Peace, in Blake Masters' notes: "you must choose your enemies well, since you'll soon
become just like them." [CS183-12] People fight because they're alike, not because they differ: "it's straight
Shakespeare. People become obsessed with their competitors." [CS183-12, from Blake Masters' class notes]

In his own words in 2024: "The conflicts are the most intense when they don't differ at all." [CWT24]

**The stories in the notes, paraphrased** [CS183-12]: Microsoft and Google were destined to fight because they
were so alike, and Apple overtook both. After Square's card reader, rivals shipped readers in other shapes; in
2014 he joked that the apes may run out of shapes [YCL5]. X.com copied PayPal feature for feature, down to the
referral bonus, and the two merged in March 2000 [CS183-12]. His own memory: "It was kind of scary to be
competiting [sic] against his startup in Palo Alto in Dec 1999-Mar 2000." [AMA14]

The notes' rule: "Focusing on your enemy is almost always the wrong thing to do." [CS183-12, from Blake Masters'
class notes] The 2014 Q&A makes the same point: defining yourself by a competitor gives up the main reason to be an
entrepreneur, which is doing something new (our paraphrase [FQA14]).

## Inside the company

The notes say most companies are killed by internal infighting, between people who want the same thing, and
that PayPal redrew its org chart every three months so nobody shared a job [CS183-12].

## Social, or seen as good?

The word social, he says, can mean "(1) good for society, or (2) seen as good by society." [AMA14] The second
"leads to me-too copycat companies." [AMA14]

## Ways out

> "I do think this idea of what's unfashionable is very important as an initial anchor." [CWT15]

> "I would always be long substance, short status." [CWT15]

He also suggests becoming "aware of how conventional our conventions really are" [CWT15]. And his honest
caveat: the first step is "to never underestimate how big a problem it is." [YCL5]

---

## How to apply it (our reading)

1. **List the five companies or people you compare yourself to most.** Note how often they come up in your team's
   meetings.
2. **For each recent decision,** ask whether you'd have made it if that rival didn't exist.
3. **Find the unfashionable version** of your idea [CWT15]: the customer, market or method your peers avoid.
4. **Check the stakes.** If you win this fight, what do you get? If it's small, you're in the Kissinger trap.
5. **Inside the team,** list any two people competing for the same job and redraw the lines [CS183-12].

**Worked scenario (fictional).** A note-taking startup's last 12 roadmap items: 8 were copies of features a
better-funded rival shipped first. Their win rate against that rival is 20%, and the deals they lose are lost on
price, not features. Removing the 8 copies frees two engineers. The unfashionable version: offline-first notes
for field technicians, a segment no rival targets. The fight they were in had small stakes; the one they weren't
in had an empty field.

**Failure modes (our reading):**
- **Contrarian by reflex.** Doing the opposite of the herd is still following it [CS183-02].
- **Copying while saying you aren't.** Check the roadmap, not the slogans.
- **Treating unfashionable as automatically right.** It's an anchor [CWT15], not a verdict.

**Limits:** much of the Girard material is from the 2012 notes; his 2024 lines are brief. The roadmap audit is
ours.

**Checks to run:**
1. Would you still want this if nobody you admire were doing anything like it?
2. Who are you competing with in your head? Has it changed what you build?
3. Is the fight you're in worth winning, or are the stakes small because the differences are small?
4. Inside your team, are two people competing for the same role? Redraw the lines.
